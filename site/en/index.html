Proofs of Okada's Conjectures 9.63 and 9.64 with constant-base-shift extensions (arXiv:2003.02112v2, Section 9.8.3)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

This manuscript asks whether subtracting two related products of symmetric polynomials can leave a symmetric coefficient sequence after zeros are discarded. In the basic, unshifted setting, Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang establish that this symmetry fails for every specified construction with at least three factors, while with two factors it survives exactly when one of two defining gaps equals one.

The building blocks are polynomial versions of binomial coefficients, called Gaussian binomial polynomials. Their coefficients read identically forward and backward; the polynomial with upper index n and lower index one is 1 + q + ⋯ + qⁿ⁻¹. Choose two strictly increasing lists of nonnegative integers, with the first list’s largest entry no greater than the second list’s smallest. Their common length is the width. Pair corresponding entries as lower and upper indices to form a product A; reverse the first list before pairing to form B. The object studied is P = A − B: what symmetry survives subtraction?

“Almost palindromic” means that the coefficient list becomes identical forward and backward after all zero entries are removed. The authors settle two conjectures from So Okada’s January 2024 manuscript. At width at least three, P starts with nonzero coefficients 1, c + 1 and ends with c, 1, where c counts A’s nonconstant factors. Deleting zeros cannot repair the mismatch. For lists (0,1,2) and (2,3,4), the nonzero list is (1,3,4,4,2,1): three and two already obstruct symmetry.

The proof compares only the ends. Writing Gaussian polynomials through products of factors 1 − qʲ allows cancellation in B/A. At low degrees, almost all factors agree; two uncancelled factors determine where cancellation stops and force the second surviving coefficient to be c + 1. At the other end, A has symmetric coefficients, and its degree exceeds B’s by at least four. Subtraction therefore leaves A’s last two coefficients, c and 1, unchanged. The authors also establish that the compared positions are distinct, without calculating the intervening coefficients.

At width two, the gaps are the differences between entries within each list. If both are at least two, the same obstruction applies; if either equals one, further cancellation proves symmetry throughout the occupied degree interval. Fixing the largest entry at an integer λ ≥ 2, counting by gaps and offsets gives the non-almost-palindromic proportion O(λ) = (λ − 2)(λ − 3)/(λ(λ + 1)). It increases strictly from λ = 3 onward and tends to one. Nevertheless, lists (0,1) and (m,n), with 1 ≤ m < n, yield a consecutive block of coefficient ones after initial zeros: infinitely many almost-palindromic examples that are also unimodal, meaning their coefficients first do not decrease and then do not increase.

A constant positive integer “base shift” changes each Gaussian factor and adds another polynomial factor; it is not merely substitution into P. At zero degree shift, B/A and the degree gap remain unchanged, so the classification and counts persist for every constant base shift. Conversely, raising both modified products to the same integer power at least two makes their difference non-almost-palindromic at every width at least two: its first nonzero coefficient equals that power, while its last is one.

A separate degree shift e multiplies only B by qᵉ. Let G be the direct-minus-reversed degree gap. Okada’s further conjecture asks whether changing the constant base shift preserves almost palindromicity for widths at least three and 0 ≤ e ≤ G. The authors prove this only at e = 0, 1, G − 1, G, and, for even G, G/2. The four outer slices are non-almost-palindromic. Reversing coefficients exchanges e and G − e, giving symmetry at the midpoint. Width two differs: extending Okada’s example, lists (0,1) and (1,n), n ≥ 4, with e = 1 lose almost palindromicity when the base shift increases from one to any larger integer. For n = 4, the initial polynomial is 1 + q² + q³, symmetric only after deleting its zero coefficient. This does not refute the width-at-least-three conjecture.

The manuscript reports exact checks of 1,486 cases of widths three through six with largest entry at most ten, and 4,845 width-two cases with largest entry at most eighteen. These finite calculations supplement written, all-parameter proofs. The reported Lean evidence covers only the unit-base, unit-weight, zero-degree-shift result at width at least three, including its endpoint coefficients—not the width-two classification, counts, or extensions. The significance is an endpoint explanation and complete two-factor classification. Other degree shifts in the base-invariance conjecture remain unresolved here. Broader unimodality questions and log-concavity, which requires each coefficient’s square to be at least the product of its neighbors, are not settled by this argument.
